# Making visual decisions

## Extract a reference's logic

When references exist, inspect them rather than relying on their reputation. Describe observable decisions: where the eye lands, how the grid changes, the relationship of type sizes, content density, image treatment, and how controls reveal their purpose. Distinguish the quality to borrow from brand assets and exact layouts. Reference admiration is not permission to copy logos, photography, or claims.

Classify each reference by its role before extracting requirements:

- **Exact target:** preserve evidenced content, layout, proportions, and behavior; disclose unobserved states and adaptations.
- **Existing system:** respect its components, tokens, terminology, and behavior within the task's scope.
- **Inspiration:** identify the qualities to borrow, such as typographic calm or image treatment; unrelated layout decisions are not requirements.
- **Source content:** use relevant facts or permitted assets without treating their presentation as a design target.

A reference can serve more than one role; specify which qualities belong to each. Check audience, task, and content-volume fit. Separate observed UI from marketing framing, platform conventions, and unshown states. Do not turn uncertain observations into fidelity claims.

Use a few discriminating questions when needed: Which part of this reference appeals to the user? What feels wrong in the current design? Is the surface for repeated daily use or occasional discovery? Avoid asking a non-designer to choose hex values and font metrics.

If references are missing, derive a direction from the subject, its content, and its audience. Any external visual research should be narrow and useful; do not browse indefinitely for a mood. When the person will choose the direction, deal the alternatives with [divergence.md](divergence.md) and give each one its detail from the subject.

## Separate independent choices

Consider these independently instead of treating “premium” as a style:

- **Information density:** How much must be compared at once? An operations table may need compact rows while the onboarding form needs space.
- **Expression:** Where can identity help without making controls unfamiliar? A distinctive canvas can coexist with ordinary form controls.
- **Tempo:** Does motion explain a transition or compete with a task? Static is often right. When movement matters, preserve access and reduced-motion behavior.
- **Material:** Does the content need photographs, diagrams, flat color, typography, or an actual product view? Choose subject-relevant material before effects.

When the whole direction is open and a person will choose it, the alternatives come from the direction round in [divergence.md](divergence.md). For a narrower consequential choice, use a small direction sketch before polishing. For uncertain composition, compare the same representative content in different structures. For styling alternatives, preserve settled structure and vary the requested qualities. A narrow edit or constrained reconstruction need not invent alternative directions.

For reference-led work, compare a rough render with the relevant reference early. Check evidenced proportions and placement for an exact target, consistency for an existing system, and only the selected qualities for inspiration. A content source is not a layout benchmark. Resolve consequential mismatches before polishing components, and check them again in the final review.

## Build a coherent visual system

Set a modest number of semantic colors: canvas, surface, text, secondary text, border, action, and relevant status roles. Test actual foreground/background pairs. Brand colors can have accessible action variants. Status meanings must also appear in text or shape.

Choose fonts for the role, reading context, language coverage, and available assets. A tuned system sans can be right for a work tool; editorial display type can carry an idea elsewhere. No font, color, radius, or symmetry is universally disallowed. If no permitted font asset is available, choose an intentional local/system stack rather than an undeclared network dependency.

Establish type roles and spacing relationships. Use proximity before adding a container: items belong together because of their task, not because a template has another card slot. Let body content wrap naturally. Test the longest plausible labels instead of hiding awkwardness with ellipses everywhere.

Keep decision-making metadata and utility labels readable at the delivered size. Subordinate text through grouping, weight and spacing before making it tiny or faint. Review the actual type and contrast pairs in context; a compact interface can remain legible, and an airy one can still have unreadable labels.

When identity or the brief benefits from expression, choose a few moments and make the rest support them. A neutral utility interface is equally valid. In either case, judge typography, proportions, rhythm, color relationships, imagery, and consistency together; working controls alone do not establish visual quality.

## Challenge the concept

- Does the composition fit the task, content, audience, and existing conventions? Reuse familiar patterns where they improve comprehension or consistency; add distinction where the brief benefits from it.
- Does an unfamiliar control add value over a standard one?
- Can the main decision be made from the information visible at that point?
- Does removing a decorative element improve focus without losing identity?
- Are unusual choices grounded in the brief, or merely reactions against another popular look?

Keep the user's explicit taste even when it is fashionable. Avoid replacing one mandatory template with another under an “anti-generic” label.
