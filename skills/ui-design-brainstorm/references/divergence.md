# Diverge before choosing

Use this when the visual direction is open and a person will choose it: a new surface with no existing system, selected concept or supplied target. A single pass produces a single answer, and for a given subject it is nearly always the same answer. Asked to pick its own alternatives, a model also returns the same few every time. Choosing needs real alternatives. So the structure of each direction is dealt, not chosen; the surface stays in the register the screen belongs to; and what makes each direction belong to this brief is written down before the deal.

1. **Write down the default.** State what you would build without thinking further: its governing metaphor, and the value you would reach for on each axis in the table below. This is direction zero. It is a reference point and is not one of the options.
2. **Choose the deck.** Use the product deck for screens inside an app or a tool: anything a person operates, decides in or reads in as part of a product. Use the expressive deck for brand-led pages, such as a landing page, a campaign or an editorial identity, or when the person asks for something bold. Say which deck and why. In the product deck the three directions differ in structure and in their cards, and the surface stays within what shipped products in the category look like.
3. **Write the subject cards.** Before dealing, write four to six cards. A card is one value for one axis that comes from this brief and is not listed in the table, with a note of its source. Draw on, in this order: the business objective the person stated; the scene of use; products or categories that do the same job for their users, in this market or another; and the subject's own world of artifacts, notation, materials and conventions. Write at least one card from each of these that the brief provides. Products and conventions the person names are sources to write cards from; a value the person asks for outright goes in as a card unchanged. From a comparable product take a convention, never a brand's look, and say whether the card comes from inspecting the product or from memory. Cards respect the deck: a product-deck card does not bring a poster surface back in. Cards are written before the deal so that none is shaped to fit a set you have already seen.
4. **Deal.** Run `python3 scripts/draw_axes.py --deck <deck> --card "<axis>=<value>"` from this skill's folder, with one `--card` for each card, and keep what it prints with the design record: the seed, the deck and the cards, in that order, reproduce the deal. It deals three sets of values for the axes below. No two sets share more than two values, the three never share a structure, and each set carries one of your cards, picked at random, in place of the dealt value on that card's axis. Cards that are not dealt sit this round out. Build each direction from its set as dealt, and find the version of an unexpected combination that serves the scene. With fewer than three cards, a set without one shows `open` on one axis; write a card for that axis then, from the subject's own world.

| Axis | Product deck | Expressive deck |
|---|---|---|
| Structure of the primary object | list or table · grid of tiles · a continuous timeline or chart that is the interface · one focal item with the rest subordinate · map or diagram | list or table · grid of tiles · a continuous timeline or chart that is the interface · one focal item with the rest subordinate · map or diagram |
| Canvas | light neutral · white · system dark mode · a softly tinted surface | light neutral · dark · a saturated color field · white with one vivid hue |
| Type | system UI sans · humanist sans · geometric sans · sans with serif headings | condensed or signage sans · geometric or humanist sans · serif-led · monospace or tabular-led |
| Scale and density | compact · comfortable · spacious with one prominent element | compact reference · comfortable · poster-scale with few elements |
| Shape language | platform-native controls · cards with clear hierarchy · ruled lists and tables · soft and rounded | ruled and square · soft and rounded · heavy blocks and fills · fine line and diagram |
| Color | neutral with one accent · status colors only · one brand color carrying identity · several hues carrying categories | status only · one brand color carrying identity · several hues carrying categories |
| Source world | the subject's own print tradition · an instrument or control surface · signage and wayfinding · editorial or magazine · a consumer product from an unrelated category | the subject's own print tradition · an instrument or control surface · signage and wayfinding · editorial or magazine · a consumer product from an unrelated category |

Reading a deal:

- The subject is the work on the screen and the world of the people doing it, more than the product's brand or its technology.
- Structure is arrangement, not style. A list in a new palette is still a list.
- Dealt values set emphasis and never remove required content. A prominent element or poster scale means one element dominates and the rest is subordinate.
- Canvas is the ground; Color is what hue is allowed to mean on it. In the expressive deck a saturated canvas can itself carry the status.
- In the product deck a source world lends structure and conventions, never a costume: signage means wayfinding logic, not a yellow screen.
- “-led” names the face that carries the identity; a supporting face is allowed.
- An unrelated consumer product lends its conventions, such as packaging, labelling and tone, and never a brand's look.
- A card changes what is seen. Renaming a listed value does not count. A card may sit on any axis, Source world included.
- The subject's own print tradition is what this work looks like on paper: its forms, labels, slips, tables and notices.
- When comparing a set with direction zero, an axis where the default has no listed value does not match, and neither does a card's axis.
- Every direction takes its wording and detail from the subject on every axis, not only on its card's.

5. **Calibrate the surface.** Before styling, name how two or three well-known products in this category present this screen: the patterns they share for layout, controls, type and density, not their branding. Say whether this comes from inspecting them or from memory. In the product deck, keep canvas, type, density and controls within that range, and let the structure and the card carry the difference. A product-deck direction should look as if it could ship in that category next month. In the expressive deck, that range is the baseline you depart from.
6. **Draw the three skeletons first.** Before any styling, sketch each direction as a small ASCII diagram: what the eye meets first, how the items are arranged, and where the actions sit. The dealt structure decides the skeleton; canvas, type and color come after it. Put the three sketches side by side. When two share an arrangement, the one that strays from its dealt structure is redrawn.
7. **Keep each one honest.** Every direction serves the same audience, scene, content and task, with the same representative content and state. When one dealt value cannot serve the scene at all, such as a canvas unreadable in the stated light, redraw that single axis once with `--deck <deck> --axis "<name>" --exclude "<value>"` and note it. Cards are not redrawn. If a set matches direction zero on five or more axes, deal again with the same cards. Never write a deal by hand. If the script cannot run, say so; the options you then show are chosen, not dealt, and are labelled that way.
8. **Show each at sketch cost.** Present each direction as one static view of the representative moment, without behavior: a rendered study, or a generated concept image when working from the image workflow. Before showing them, look at the three with the color removed, as greyscale copies or small thumbnails side by side. When two read as the same layout in different palettes, rebuild the one that strays from its dealt structure. Name each in a few words. List the deck, the values it was dealt, its card and where the card came from, and any redraw; keep its skeleton sketch with the design record; and add one sentence on what it gains and what it risks.
9. **Let the person choose.** Present the set and stop. They may pick one, combine several, redirect, add or strike cards, switch decks, or ask for another deal. Record the seed, the deck, the cards, the three directions, what they chose and what they said in the project's design record. A later deal for the same project starts from that record and leaves out combinations they have already seen.
10. **Build from the choice.** Where the chosen study is code, start the build from it. Carry its structure and visual language into the working result, and keep direction zero's choices out of the places where they picked something else. Say where the working result departs from the study and why.

When nobody is available to choose, as in an unattended run, deal as above, build the direction that best serves the scene, and record that the choice was yours.

Skip this for refinements, extensions of an existing system, briefs that already fix the direction, and requests for one quick result. In the last case, say that a direction round is available.
