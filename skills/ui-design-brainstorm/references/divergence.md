# Diverge before choosing

Use this when the visual direction is open and a person will choose it: a new surface with no existing system, selected concept or supplied target. A single pass produces a single answer, and for a given subject it is nearly always the same answer. Asked to pick its own alternatives, a model also returns the same few every time. Choosing needs real alternatives, so the directions are dealt, not chosen. The deal is anchored in how this category's products actually look, and what makes each direction belong to this brief is written down before the deal.

1. **Write down the default.** State what you would build without thinking further: its governing metaphor, and the value you would reach for on each axis of the deck you will use. This is direction zero. It is a reference point and is not one of the options.
2. **Choose the deck.** Use the product deck for screens inside an app or a tool: anything a person operates, decides in or reads in as part of a product. Use the expressive deck for brand-led pages, such as a landing page, a campaign or an editorial identity, or when the person asks for something bold. Say which deck and why. Products in one category share a layout and differ inside it. So in the product deck, two directions keep the category's usual layout and differ in what leads, how each item reads and what they borrow; the third, the stretch, is dealt a different structure. Canvas, type and controls follow the category in all three.
3. **Calibrate against the category.** Before writing cards, look at how two or three products in this category present this screen. When you can browse or search, inspect them: store screenshots, help-centre articles and product pages show real screens. Otherwise work from memory, and say which you did for each. Write down what they share: the screen's layout as a small ASCII sketch, the listed structure it is closest to, the platform chrome (navigation, search, sort and filter, date or tab controls, a bottom action bar), and the usual canvas, type and density. Then note what each does differently inside that layout: what leads, how one item carries its data, and the one feature tied to its business. That is where products in a category differ, and it is what the grounded directions vary. Take conventions, never a brand's look. In the expressive deck this is the baseline you depart from.
4. **Write the subject cards.** Write four to six cards. A card is one value for one axis that comes from this brief and is not listed in the table, with a note of its source. Draw on, in this order: the business objective the person stated; the scene of use; the products from step 3, and apps in other categories that handle a similar moment; and the subject's own world of artifacts, notation, materials and conventions. Write at least one card from each of these that the brief provides. Products and conventions the person names are sources to write cards from. A value the person asks for outright goes in as a card unchanged; in the product deck, a request about canvas or type applies to all three directions instead. From another product take a convention, never a brand's look, and say whether the card comes from inspecting the product or from memory. Cards respect the deck. A product-deck card sits on any product axis except structure. A card drawn from paper, print or signage lends its logic and never its look: a par sheet gives the order of the items, not ruled paper or typewriter type. Cards are written before the deal so that none is shaped to fit a set you have already seen.
5. **Deal.** From this skill's folder, run `python3 scripts/draw_axes.py --usual "<structure>" --card "<axis>=<value>"` for the product deck, with `--usual` naming the listed structure closest to the layout from step 3. For the expressive deck, run `python3 scripts/draw_axes.py --deck expressive --card "<axis>=<value>"`. Pass one `--card` for each card, and keep what the script prints with the design record. The seed, the deck, the usual structure and the cards, in that order, reproduce the deal.

   The script deals three sets of values for the axes below. No two sets share more than two values. In the product deck, two sets keep the category's usual layout and share no other value, the stretch is dealt a structure other than the usual one, and no two sets share a lead or an item anatomy. In the expressive deck, the three never share a structure or a canvas. Each set carries one of your cards, picked at random, in place of the dealt value on that card's axis; cards that are not dealt sit this round out. Build each direction from its set as dealt, and find the version of an unexpected combination that serves the scene. With fewer than three cards, a set without one shows `open` on one axis; write a card for that axis then.

| Axis | Product deck |
|---|---|
| Structure of the primary object | list or table · grid of tiles · a continuous timeline or chart that is the interface · one focal item with the rest subordinate · map or diagram |
| Lead | the recommended choice · what needs an answer · the limit being worked to · the running total or progress · the content itself |
| Item anatomy | one line with a trailing value · two lines with labels or badges · an inline bar or timeline in each item · choices inline in each item · collapsed items that expand in place |
| Scale and density | compact · comfortable · spacious with one prominent element |
| Shape language | platform-native controls · cards with clear hierarchy · ruled lists and tables · soft and rounded |
| Color | neutral with one accent · status colors only · one brand color carrying identity · several hues carrying categories |
| Borrowed from another category | a banking or payments app · a grocery or food delivery app · a messaging app · a maps or navigation app · a calendar or booking app · a review and approval tool · a fitness or health tracker |

In the product deck only the stretch is dealt a structure, and never the usual one; the other two show `the category's usual layout`.

| Axis | Expressive deck |
|---|---|
| Structure of the primary object | list or table · grid of tiles · a continuous timeline or chart that is the interface · one focal item with the rest subordinate · map or diagram |
| Canvas | light neutral · dark · a saturated color field · white with one vivid hue |
| Type | condensed or signage sans · geometric or humanist sans · serif-led · monospace or tabular-led |
| Scale and density | compact reference · comfortable · poster-scale with few elements |
| Shape language | ruled and square · soft and rounded · heavy blocks and fills · fine line and diagram |
| Color | status only · one brand color carrying identity · several hues carrying categories |
| Source world | the subject's own print tradition · an instrument or control surface · signage and wayfinding · editorial or magazine · a consumer product from an unrelated category |

Reading a deal:

- The subject is the work on the screen and the world of the people doing it, more than the product's brand or its technology.
- Structure is arrangement, not style. A list in a new palette is still a list.
- The category's usual layout is the sketch from step 3, chrome included. A grounded direction keeps it and changes what happens inside it.
- Lead is what the eye meets first in the primary area. Item anatomy is how one item in the primary object carries its data, applied to every item.
- Borrowed from another category lends one pattern from an app outside this category that handles a similar moment: a delivery app's substitution approval, a banking app's pending items, a review tool's comments pinned to a time. Name the pattern you take. If the dealt category is the subject's own, redraw that axis once.
- Dealt values set emphasis and never remove required content. A prominent element or poster scale means one element dominates and the rest is subordinate.
- In the expressive deck, Canvas is the ground and Color is what hue is allowed to mean on it; a saturated canvas can itself carry the status. “-led” names the face that carries the identity, and a supporting face is allowed.
- A source world lends conventions such as forms, labelling and tone, never a costume. The subject's own print tradition is what this work looks like on paper: its forms, labels, slips, tables and notices.
- A card changes what is seen. Renaming a listed value does not count.
- When comparing a set with direction zero, an axis where the default has no listed value does not match, and neither does a card's axis.
- Every direction takes its wording and detail from the subject on every axis, not only on its card's.

6. **Draw the three skeletons first.** Before any styling, sketch each direction as a small ASCII diagram: what the eye meets first, how the items are arranged, and where the actions sit. The two grounded directions start from the step 3 sketch. Their sketches differ in at least two of these: where the lead sits and how much room it takes, such as a band above the items, a marker among them, a side panel or a bar pinned to the bottom; the silhouette of one item, such as one line, two lines with badges, a bar, or a row of choices; and how the items are grouped and ordered. A different label in the same box does not count. The stretch follows its dealt structure. Put the three sketches side by side. Redraw the stretch if it falls back into the usual layout, and redraw a grounded sketch if the two could be swapped without anyone noticing.
7. **Keep each one honest.** Every direction serves the same audience, scene, content and task, with the same representative content and state. When one dealt value cannot serve the scene at all, redraw that single axis once and note it, using `--axis "<name>" --exclude "<value>"` with the same deck flags; for the stretch's structure, add `--usual`. Cards are not redrawn. If a set matches direction zero on five or more axes, deal again with the same cards. Never write a deal by hand. If the script cannot run, say so; the options you then show are chosen, not dealt, and are labelled that way.
8. **Show each at sketch cost.** Present each direction as one static view of the representative moment, without behavior: a rendered study, or a generated concept image when working from the image workflow. A product-deck study looks like a screen from a shipped app. It carries the chrome step 3 found, without inventing data for it, and its canvas, type and controls are the category's. Before showing them, render the three without color and look at them small, side by side. This is a pass or fail test, not a matter of preference. If the two grounded directions could be taken for the same screen at that size, rebuild the one that strays further from its deal: move its lead to a different place or give it a different weight, and change the silhouette of its items. Then test again. Rebuild the stretch if it reads as the usual layout. Do not show a set that fails, and record that the test was run. Name each direction in a few words and mark the stretch. List the deck, the values it was dealt, its card and where the card came from, and any redraw. Keep its skeleton sketch with the design record, and add one sentence on what it gains and what it risks.
9. **Let the person choose.** Present the set and stop. They may pick one, combine several, redirect, add or strike cards, switch decks, or ask for another deal. In the project's design record, write down the products from step 3, the seed, the deck, the usual structure, the cards, the three directions, what they chose and what they said. A later deal for the same project starts from that record and leaves out combinations they have already seen.
10. **Build from the choice.** Where the chosen study is code, start the build from it. Carry its structure and visual language into the working result, and keep direction zero's choices out of the places where they picked something else. Say where the working result departs from the study and why.

When nobody is available to choose, as in an unattended run, deal as above, build the direction that best serves the scene, and record that the choice was yours.

Skip this for refinements, extensions of an existing system, briefs that already fix the direction, and requests for one quick result. In the last case, say that a direction round is available.
